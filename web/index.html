Your agent retried.
Do you know what it already did?

Ratchet is how you let an agent do things it cannot take back — charge a card, ship a deploy, publish a package, send the email — and know what it already did, what that cost, and what it may still spend.

  • Never twice. A retry, a restart and two workers racing all get the same answer.
  • Never a guess. When nobody can know whether it happened, we say so instead of assuming.
  • It remembers for you. An agent that lost its context asks what it already did — one call, not a re-derivation.
  • It cannot overspend. Give a task a budget and the gate refuses the step that would cross it.

The problem is not that agents fail. It's that they retry.

An LLM's control flow is non-deterministic. Network calls fail ambiguously. Processes die mid-action. So the same logical action gets attempted zero, one, or several times — and nothing in your stack knows which.

The timeout that wasn't

Your agent posts a charge. The connection drops before the response. Did it go through? The agent guesses, retries, and the customer is billed twice.

The helpful second try

A supervisor loop notices a subtask “didn't finish” and re-runs it. The subtask had already sent the announcement to 4,000 people.

The parallel worker

Two agents pick up the same queue item a second apart. Both check “has this been done?” in their own memory. Both see no, and both act.

Vendor idempotency keys help for the handful of vendors that offer them. They do nothing for SMTP, Slack, git, internal APIs, or the other twenty tools your agent touches — and nothing at all across separate processes.

What a repeat actually costs

Not knowing is the expensive part

An agent that cannot tell whether it already charged the card has three options, and all three cost you.

Do it again

The customer is charged twice. You refund it, eat the fee, and answer the email. The vendor already took the money — the mistake is real before anyone notices.

Don't

The work silently never completes. Nobody finds out until the person who was waiting for it complains, which is the most expensive way to learn anything.

Go and find out

Query the vendor, re-read your own state, hand it back to the model to decide. This is the loop that burns tokens and minutes — on every ambiguous action, for as long as the agent runs.

The gate replaces all three with one call. A repeat is answered from the recorded outcome, which is why the interesting number is not how fast the gate decides but how much work it removes: the third option above is a round trip to a vendor plus a model turn, and it becomes a single lookup.

And your console adds it up. What this does not do. Ratchet never sees your model calls. It does not make inference cheaper, prompts shorter, or a model faster, and anyone telling you a gate does that is selling something. What it removes is the work you would otherwise do to find out what already happened.

New

A run gets a memory, and a wallet

An agent's memory of what it has done lives in its context window — the worst possible place to keep it. Compaction summarises it away, every token of it is paid for again each turn, and it cannot be checked: an agent can sincerely believe it sent the email and be wrong.

Ask what you already did

One call returns the work already gated under a task: what succeeded, with the recorded results, so you use them instead of doing it again. What is still in flight. And — kept separate, because it is the only category that can hurt — what has an outcome nobody knows. 

GET /v1/runs/trip-8812

{ "done": [ { "what": "flight.book", "outcome": "succeeded" } ],
  "unknown": [],
  "budget": { "remaining_micros": 140000 },
  "next": "Everything under this run is settled." }

Give the task a budget

This job may spend fifty dollars was a sentence nobody could say. Daily limits bind a key or an effect type; neither is a task. Now a run carries its own wallet, and the gate refuses the step that would cross it — before the money moves, which is the only moment refusing is still cheap. 

PUT /v1/runs/trip-8812/budget
{ "limit_micros": 50000000 }

The half that matters is not the ceiling. Budgets could always refuse. It is that the agent can read its own remaining balance — and an agent that can see what is left behaves differently from one that can only be stopped. It takes the cheaper path, batches the work, asks for approval, or finishes early and says why. Being refused at the moment of spending is the worst possible time to learn a limit exists.

An agent cannot raise its own ceiling. Reading the balance needs the scope agents carry; setting it needs one they do not, and there is deliberately no tool for it in the MCP toolbox. A limit the agent can lift is decoration.

Measured on a real run: a full effect record is 864 bytes, so a twelve-step task listed in full is 10.1KB of context. The digest is 0.6KB — about seventeen times smaller. That ratio is the point, because a memory costing more than re-deriving the answer does not get used.

Ask before you act

One call before the action, one call after. Ratchet holds the durable record.

Ratchet never performs the action. It has no access to your vendors, your credentials, or your payload — only a hash of it.

Six answers. Only one means go.

execute You hold the lease. Perform the action, then report.
duplicate Already done. Replay the recorded result. Don't act.
in_flight Another process holds a live lease. Back off.
blocked A prior attempt's outcome is unknown. Verify first.
approval_required An operator must approve this one.
denied Policy or budget refused it.

The part that makes it worth paying for

When we don't know, we say so.

If your process dies between “go” and “done”, most systems quietly let the next caller retry. Ratchet won't. The lease expires and the effect becomes indeterminate — a known unknown, surfaced instead of buried.

block

The default, and the right choice for anything irreversible. No automatic retry. A human or a verifying agent resolves it after checking what really happened.

retry

For actions where the vendor is genuinely idempotent, or a duplicate is harmless. You opt in per effect type — never by default, because we can't know that for you.

probe

The caller must verify the outcome at the vendor and record evidence before anything else may proceed. For charges, transfers, and anything you'd have to apologise for.

Exactly-once delivery is not achievable in a distributed system, and we won't claim it. What Ratchet guarantees is at-most-once initiation, a recorded outcome that later callers replay, and an explicit state for the case nobody else admits exists.

Signed receipts

Don't trust us. Check us.

A gate's work is invisible. The charge that didn't happen leaves no trace, and "trust us, we stopped it" is unfalsifiable — a bad place to stand for a product whose whole pitch is rigour.

So every decision now leaves a signed receipt, and refusals most of all. Verify it offline against a published Ed25519 key — no need to ask us anything. Receipts are hash-chained, so removing one breaks every link after it. GET /v1/receipts/audit recomputes the whole chain from stored bytes and trusts none of its own columns.

The failure we can't see from here

A gate only sees the calls that reach it. The path in your system that never asked — the old cron job, the script nobody owns — is invisible to us and to you. POST /v1/reconcile closes that: send the keys your vendor says it acted on, and we tell you which ones we authorised. 

POST /v1/reconcile  { "effect_type": "payment.refund", "keys": [ … ] }

→ { "checked": 1204, "gated": 1198, "ungated": 6,
    "meaning": "6 action(s) reached the vendor without ever asking Ratchet.
                Those code paths are unprotected: a retry there can act twice." }

You send references, never credentials. Ratchet still holds no vendor access, and this doesn't change that.

A ceiling nothing counts toward isn't a ceiling

Spend limits are computed from the cost you declare on each call. Declare nothing and the check doesn't merely under-count — it exits immediately. You can configure a $100/day limit, believe you're protected, and have it never fire once.

So send estimated_cost_micros whenever an action costs money. If a limit exists and you didn't, the response now carries a budget_warning saying so — and setting require_cost on a policy refuses the call outright, turning a silent gap into an explicit decision.

What it actually saved

GET /v1/usage/prevented counts the duplicates refused and what they would have cost. It counts only refusals where you declared a cost up front, so it under-reports rather than flatters — and it's money not spent at your vendors, never revenue paid to us.

New: surge containment

The loop that costs nothing is the one that ruins you

Spend limits catch an agent burning money. They don't catch an agent doing too much — and that failure is usually free. Five thousand emails instead of three costs about a dollar. Every one of them is small, authorised, and indistinguishable from correct work.

No per-effect limit can see it, because nothing is wrong with any single effect. Only the rate gives it away. Ratchet sits at the one place that sees the rate — every intended effect passes through the gate before it happens — so the surge gets caught while it's still three emails in. 

PUT /v1/policies/email.send  { "surge_per_hour": 400 }

# 400/hour is fine here. 401 means something is looping.

→ { "decision": "approval_required",
    "reason":   "Circuit breaker open for \"email.send\": 401 effects
                 this hour exceeds the configured ceiling of 400." }

Notice what that decision isn't. The agent isn't killed — it's held. A blocked agent loses its work and its context; a waiting one keeps both while a human decides, and nothing irreversible happens in between. Choose deny if you really do want it stopped dead, or monitor to watch for a week before enforcing anything.

And when you need the big red button, it's one call: POST /v1/circuits/*/open halts every effect type in the workspace. A breaker you open by hand has no cooldown — it stays open until you close it, because a control you reached for in a panic must not quietly undo itself while you sleep.

Off until you set a threshold. Agent keys can't close a breaker — an agent that can switch off its own containment hasn't been contained.

Vendor-enforced idempotency

A gate only stops the agents that ask

Here is the weakness every product in this category shares, and most don't mention. A well-behaved agent asks permission. The agent you're actually afraid of — the one in a retry loop, the cron job someone wrote last year, the colleague's script — just doesn't call. Ask-first protection is advisory, and advisory protection fails exactly when you need it.

So don't rely on being asked. When Ratchet authorises an effect it now hands back the idempotency key the vendor itself deduplicates on. You pass it to Stripe, or Square, or Adyen. The duplicate is then refused by the vendor — not prevented by your agent's good manners, and not dependent on anything reaching us at all. 

POST /v1/effects/begin   { "effect_type": "payment.charge",
                           "idempotency_key": "refund:A-771:49.99",
                           "vendor": "stripe" }

→ { "decision": "execute",
    "vendor_idempotency_key": {
      "key":       "rtk_9f2c…",
      "placement": "Idempotency-Key header",
      "enforced":  true } }

# Send that key to Stripe. Stripe now refuses the second charge itself.

The key is derived per attempt, which is the part that matters. Retrying the same attempt reuses it, so the vendor refuses the duplicate. A genuine retry after a reported failure gets a fresh key, so the vendor doesn't replay the old failure forever and leave the effect permanently stuck.

Composed with a vendor that honours idempotency keys, at-most-once initiation becomes at-most-once execution at that vendor. Where a vendor does not deduplicate, the response says enforced: false and the gate stays advisory — we'd rather tell you than let you assume. The free vendor directory lists which is which, no account needed — as a page or as JSON.

Reversible effect groups

Undo is the hard part

At-most-once tells you whether something happened. It does not help when three of five steps happened and the fourth failed — and your customer is holding a flight for a trip that was never paid for.

  • Reverse order, always. The last thing done is the first thing undone, because undoing forward strands whatever depended on it.
  • Every undo is itself gated. A compensation is just another effect, so a retried rollback cannot double-refund. That is the failure that makes hand-rolled rollback dangerous.
  • It refuses to lie. A step that declared no way to undo itself is reported irreversible, and the group settles unwind_failed rather than claiming a clean rollback. An email cannot be unsent.
  • Unknowns block the plan. If any step's outcome is uncertain, the rollback stops until you verify it. Rolling back past a maybe is how half-undone states are made.

Also, while we're holding the gate

Spend ceilings that actually bind

Declare what an action costs you. Ratchet reserves it against per-key, per-effect-type, and per-workspace daily budgets — atomically, so twenty parallel workers cannot collectively blow a limit that each of them individually respects.

Approval gates without a human in every loop

Mark an effect type require_approval and only that type pauses. Everything else keeps running unattended. Configure once; no per-call babysitting.

An audit trail you didn't have to build

Every decision, lease, outcome, and resolution is recorded with the agent and run that caused it. When someone asks “did we email them twice?”, there is an answer.

Fencing tokens, not just locks

A stalled worker that wakes up after its lease expired cannot overwrite the newer attempt's result. It's told its lease was superseded, which is what it needed to know.

Paying for it

Card or crypto, never custody

Subscribe with a card, or top up prepaid credit with USDC on Solana. Crypto is non-custodial: payments go straight to an address the operator controls and Ratchet only watches the chain. It holds no key and takes custody of nothing.

Quotes are struck in USD and the credit granted is the USD amount, so a price move between quote and settlement cannot mint credit that was not paid for. Volatile assets are refused rather than quoted at a guessed price — that is a treasury decision, not an engineering one.
